Before you start

Have a treat or a real toy ready. This is the part most screen games and laser pointers get wrong. A cat's hunting sequence ends in a physical catch, and a hunt that never ends leaves them wound up. When the timer runs out, give them something they can actually pin down.

Put a screen protector on. Claws come out. A tempered glass sheet costs a few pounds and saves the tablet.

Lay the tablet flat and hold it still, or wedge it against something. A sliding screen breaks the illusion instantly.

Some cats won't register. Dry paw pads and fur don't always trigger a capacitive screen, and plenty of cats simply aren't interested in screens — I've seen no reliable figure for how many, so don't trust anyone who gives you one. Two minutes of watching tells you which kind you have.

Why it looks like this
Yellow and blue, never red
Most studies find two cone types, peaking around 460 and 560 nanometres, so their colour world runs blue to yellow-green. Long wavelengths land as dim and washed out — a red dot reads to them as a grey smudge they track only by its motion. Every creature here is painted in the range a cat can genuinely separate from the background.
Tap Cat vision on the preview to run it through a deuteranope simulation and check for yourself that the quarry still stands out.
Big shapes, no fine detail
Feline visual acuity sits somewhere near 20/100 to 20/200 against human 20/20. Detail is wasted. The creatures are drawn large, in flat high-contrast blocks with a soft halo, because edge contrast and brightness are what a cat's eye resolves at a metre away.
Dark ground
A cat's retina is rod-heavy and built for low light, with a reflective tapetum behind it. A near-black field lets the quarry read as a bright moving object rather than a shape lost in glare, and it's easier on their eyes than a white background.
It stops. Then it bolts.
Constant smooth motion habituates fast. What drives the stalk-and-pounce sequence is the freeze: prey goes still, the cat closes distance, then it breaks.
There is no pattern to learn
The commonest reason a cat gives up on something like this is that they've worked out the rhythm. So each move is drawn from a repertoire rather than repeated: ordinary darts, panic sprints, sudden doubling back, zigzag runs, flinching false starts and the occasional long dead stop. On top of that every session rolls the quarry a fresh temperament, shifting its speed, its patience and how sharply it turns, so the same creature never plays the same way twice. If she's lost interest, switch on Shuffle and let it rotate creatures mid-session.
The floor answers back
Leaves lie scattered across the ground and tumble away from a paw, tufts shake when they're struck, and a miss still kicks up grit. A cat that swipes and gets nothing at all learns the screen is inert; making the terrain respond keeps a near miss worth something. All of it is drawn dim on purpose — brightness contrast is what a cat's eye resolves, so busy scenery would cost the quarry its salience.
It goes to ground
Open-floor looping is a video game; real prey breaks for cover and waits. Each session scatters a few tufts across the screen, and the quarry will bolt into one and sit tight with just enough movement showing to hold a stare. Pouncing the tuft counts as a catch — the whole patch is the target while it's hiding — and a paw landing nearby flushes it out at speed.
It always runs away
Nothing edible moves toward a predator. Put a paw down near the quarry and it breaks away from the contact point, which is why the fleeing burst is the fastest movement in the game.
It gets bored before your cat does
If nothing touches the screen for twenty-five seconds the quarry slows right down, holds its freezes longer and twitches in place instead of running. That's the same thing you'd do with a wand toy when a cat's attention drifts: less movement, not more. The last forty-five seconds of a session it tires steadily, so play is likely to finish on a catch rather than break off mid-chase.
It can be caught
The one rule that matters. Each near miss slows the quarry slightly and widens its catch radius, so pursuit reliably converts. The reasoning: a cat's predatory sequence ends in a physical catch, and a 2021 owner survey found frequency of laser-pointer play associated with almost every repetitive behaviour it measured. That study is correlational and self-reported, so treat it as a good reason for caution rather than a proven mechanism.

Everything runs in this page — nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. Screen play is a supplement to real toys, not a replacement for them.
